# Find your brand’s angle

A **niche** is a particular person, situation, and need you choose to serve. Your site should help that person answer: **“Why is this for me?”**

Reuse your earlier **design library and Brand Studio** work. This is part of Research and Plan, not another assignment or report. Keep decisions in your existing project record or `SPEC.md` and link your style guide.

## Compare two possible niches

Keep the product plain white. You can change whom you help and what you emphasize without inventing better fabric or adding custom printing.

| | Option 1: straightforward purchase | Option 2: creative wardrobe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Situation | A commuter buying one everyday tee | A student building outfits around one plain tee |
| Need to explore | Clear fit and total cost without bundle decisions | Ideas for making a simple item feel personal |
| Possible promise | One everyday tee. Clear fit. One straightforward price. | Your everyday blank canvas. |
| Possible character | Everyperson: practical and welcoming | Creator: inventive and specific |
| Useful response | Measurements near sizes; itemized total | Original styling examples; clear product facts |

These are **persona hypotheses**, not discovered customer facts. Ask about a real recent purchase: What were they trying to do? What information mattered? Compare need, evidence, and what you can reasonably build; choose one angle. Three websites and one classmate do not prove market demand.

Write one sentence: **For [person in a situation], our [offer] helps with [need]. We emphasize [credible difference]. We still need to check [assumption].**

A persona describes the **user**. An archetype describes your **brand’s character**. Your niche says **why this offer matters**. They are related, but different.

## Make the brand visible

Choose one archetype and three voice words. Show them in actual wording and visual choices; writing “Creator” in the plan is not enough.

Then compare two small visual directions for your chosen offer. Reuse Brand Studio previews. For a fair visual comparison, hold the **product, price, headline, supporting copy, and main action** steady. Change the visual treatment. These previews can grow into your existing two working versions; do not build two extra sites.

| Modernist-inspired route | Postmodernist-inspired route |
| --- | --- |
| Explore a specific source’s grid, hierarchy, geometry, and relationship between form and purpose. | Explore a specific source’s juxtaposition, unexpected relationships, historical quotation, or expressive composition. |
| Example source: Josef Müller-Brockmann, *Musica Viva*, 1957, [MoMA](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/4885). | Example source: Ettore Sottsass, *Carlton* room divider, 1981, [The Met](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/486989). |

Open and inspect the actual work. Name one idea you translated and why it suits the audience. Modernism does not simply mean plain, and postmodernism does not simply mean colorful. Both routes still need readable text, clear controls, and keyboard access. [V&A: Modernism](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/what-was-modernism) · [Postmodernism](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/what-is-postmodernism/).

The class samples compare visual treatment **and information order**. Their shared copy makes the offer consistent, but they do not isolate the cause of a preference. You may compare another meaningful design decision instead; name what changed and keep the task consistent.

## Give honest reasons to act

Carry forward **two useful persuasion applications** from Brand Studio. Explain the element, proposed mechanism, and evidence or limit. You do not need all seven principles or proof of increased sales.

<details>
<summary>Cialdini’s seven principles: quick checks and mistakes to avoid</summary>

| Principle | What to look for or try | Check the claim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reciprocity | Useful guidance before asking for an action | A free guide offers value; it does not prove more purchases. |
| Scarcity | A real, documented limit | A sale alone is not scarcity. Do not invent stock or countdowns. |
| Authority | Relevant qualifications or demonstrated work | A polished photo or badge is not evidence of expertise. |
| Consistency | A voluntary commitment followed by a related action | Required size selection is not a commitment mechanism by itself. |
| Liking | Sincere, relatable language and relevant similarities | Explain the intended connection; do not claim a measured effect. |
| Social proof | Authentic responses from other people | Do not invent testimonials or copy review counts. |
| Unity | A genuine shared identity | Being near NJIT does not imply NJIT endorsement. |

Use [Robert Cialdini’s explanations](https://www.influenceatwork.com/7-principles-of-persuasion/) to check the mechanism. Keep fictional businesses clearly labeled. An AI customer cannot provide authentic reviews or real market validation.

</details>

## Turn the promise into something testable

**Promise:** clear fit before purchase. **Story:** “As a shopper unsure about fit, I want measurements so I can choose a size.” **Feature:** guide beside sizes. **Check:** a peer finds it without a hint, reads the units, and chooses a size.

Ask a peer: **What is offered? Who is it for? What gives you a reason to trust it? What would you do next?** Then let them attempt the main task before you explain. Record an actual observation, make a useful change, and retest. “They liked the colors” is weaker evidence than “they understood the offer and found the size guide.”

For a **group service**, narrow the customer situation and explain the next step. For a **portfolio**, choose a main reader and show real project evidence. [See six completed examples and rubric feedback](/examples/submissions/).
