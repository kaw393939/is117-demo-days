# Class example: the plain white T-shirt

**Question:** How can a new brand distinguish a plain white T-shirt and communicate that difference through its website?

This example compares four men's white crew-neck shirts, researched November 2, 2026. The companies have larger catalogs; the selected product pages are references for a focused one-product business. Audience descriptions and design assessments are interpretations. Material/performance descriptions are brand claims, not independent test results.

| Brand / primary source | Regular or list price | Observed one-time single price | Positioning / product facts | Design observation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Fresh Clean Tees](https://freshcleantees.com/products/white-crew-neck) | $23 | $23 | Everyday fit/comfort; 60% cotton, 40% polyester; short/regular/tall | Model measurements and fit options support selection |
| [True Classic](https://www.trueclassictees.com/products/white-crew-neck-tee) | $34.99 | $33.99 promotion | Athletic fit; 60% cotton, 40% polyester; length/hem/pack choices | Many choices and pricing paths create decisions to explain |
| [Buck Mason](https://www.buckmason.com/products/white-slub-curved-hem-tee) | $48 | $48 | USA-grown cotton; Pennsylvania manufacture; textured cotton/slub cotton | Specific production context supports premium positioning |
| [Cuts](https://www.cutsclothing.com/products/white-crew-curve-hem) | $54 | $38 sale | Professional use case; 62% polyester, 33% cotton, 5% spandex | Flat and worn photos; fabric benefits tied to daily use |

Prices are USD before tax/shipping. Membership, subscription, and pack prices are not used in the one-time comparison. True Classic displayed an optional $1/month membership; Cuts showed separate subscription pricing. Prices and variant availability change.

## Interpretation

Two shirts list the same fiber proportions, so composition alone does not explain the offer. Fit, evidence, brand story, and the shopping experience also matter. Buck Mason emphasizes provenance; Cuts explains a professional use case. See the linked product sources above and [screenshots with detailed observations](/examples/site-references).

## Compare the brand, not just its features

These are interpretations of the linked pages, not official archetype declarations or independent verification of claims.

| Brand | Look and character | Reasons to trust or act | What we might do differently |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fresh Clean Tees | Photo-led functional layout; approachable everyday voice suggests **Everyperson**. | Relatable models may support liking; displayed reviews are social-proof cues. | Focus on one buying situation and put fit information close to the choice. |
| True Classic | Bold identity and many comparisons; **Everyperson**, with aspirational **Hero** elements, is a possible reading. | Ratings/customer counts are displayed social-proof claims. Sale pricing alone does not establish scarcity. | Make a single-tee purchase understandable without bundle or membership decisions. |
| Buck Mason | Restrained editorial presentation and production context suggest **Creator/craftsmanship**. | Specific production details may signal relevant competence; appearance alone does not establish authority. | Give specific facts we can support. Do not invent a mill story or provenance. |
| Cuts | Product/worn photos and a professional-use story suggest **Hero/Ruler** elements. | Displayed reviews provide social-proof cues; the workday identity may support liking. Subscription choices alone are not consistency. | Explain the use case visually and make the one-time total clear. |

Most of these pages use contemporary functional commerce layouts. We cannot establish historical Modernism from a grid alone or force a Postmodernism label onto one competitor. Use a specific historical source when developing your own visual routes.

**The strategic question:** Which customer situation can we serve clearly with the same plain white product? Compare a straightforward single purchase with a creative wardrobe starting point. These are possible niches to investigate, not proven gaps. [See the two niche cards and design exercise](/process/find-your-angle).

## Example opportunity hypothesis

A straightforward everyday white tee for college students, with clear measurements, useful photography, and one understandable price. Explore $25 as a classroom concept price. This is a hypothesis, not proof of market demand, manufacturing feasibility, or profitability.

## Turn findings into decisions

| Observation | Proposed feature | Reason | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fit information appears across competitors | Measurement guide beside sizes | Reduce uncertainty before selection | A peer can locate it and explain their size choice |
| Multiple pricing paths are common | Clearly labeled single price and itemized summary | Make the offer understandable | Peer identifies price; quantity totals calculate correctly |
| Photography explains shape and fit | Front/back/detail and worn views | Show what words cannot | Peer can distinguish hem, fit, and fabric detail |
| Brands explain their product's difference | Specific audience and honest brand story | Give the shirt a meaningful position | Peer can describe who it serves and why |

The [worked specification](/examples/tshirt-spec) continues this example. Students should develop their own audience and decisions, not copy the example brand.
