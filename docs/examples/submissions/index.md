## What to look for

Every example includes a source-linked comparison of three websites, two persona hypotheses, two short website approaches, user stories and acceptance checks, two clickable working versions, a launch decision, a sample peer-feedback/change record, QA prompts, a case study, an AI-use statement, and a final submission checklist.

The group examples also include equal-work plans and two individual contribution statements. The portfolio examples include two complete sets of résumé content, GitHub profile README text, LinkedIn drafts, project case studies, and short demo scripts.

## Why two?

A second credible alternative helps you notice assumptions and explain your choice. **Compare two options, choose, then move forward.** Do not branch into an ever-growing set of projects. Versions can share code and the core workflow; their positioning, information order, or interaction should differ meaningfully. Changing only a color is not a useful comparison.

For competitive research, make **one shared comparison table**, then consider two possible positions. For personas, compare two needs/audiences. For specifications, compare two concise ways to serve the selected audience. For the product, compare two working versions.

## Use the examples honestly

Businesses and students are fictional. Source-page observations are distinguished from assumptions. Sample peer feedback, credentials, and contribution statements are illustrative; they are not real research or student evidence. Do your own walkthrough and document at least one meaningful improvement for **each** project.

The public demos use browser storage. A [downloadable local version](/examples/submissions/run-examples) demonstrates SQLite-backed records. Portfolio profile buttons open clearly labeled drafts; replace them with your own account links in your submission.
